In the hours after a fire, everyone tells you what you should feel. We would rather tell you what to do. This is the list we walk families through, in order.

## First: people

Make sure everyone, including pets, is accounted for and gets checked out. Smoke inhalation symptoms can show up hours later. If anyone was near the fire, get them evaluated.

Do not go back inside until the fire department says the structure is safe. Floors can be weakened, ceilings can drop, and smoldering can restart. This is true even for "small" kitchen fires.

## Within the first few hours

**Call your insurance company.** Report the loss, get a claim number, and ask about Additional Living Expenses coverage if you cannot stay in the home. Most policies pay for a hotel and meals. Ask before you spend.

**Get a copy of the fire report.** The responding department will produce one. Your carrier will want it.

**Secure the property.** Broken windows, a burned door, a hole in the roof: the house is now open to weather, animals and people. Your policy requires you to protect it from further damage. We provide emergency board-up and roof tarping the same day, and it is billed to the claim. Call (814) 809-9833.

**Contact utilities.** Do not turn gas, electric or water back on yourself. Have the utility or a licensed contractor check the systems first.

**Tell the people who need to know:** your mortgage company, your employer, your kids' school, the post office.

## What to take out of the house (if you are cleared to enter)

- Identification, insurance documents, checkbooks, credit cards
- Medications and eyeglasses
- Irreplaceable items: photos, heirlooms, hard drives
- Pets' supplies

Wear a mask and gloves. Soot is acidic and the air is full of particulates. Do not sit on furniture or touch walls more than you have to.

## What not to do

- **Do not wipe walls, ceilings or furniture.** Soot smears and sets.
- **Do not run the HVAC.** It spreads soot through every duct and room.
- **Do not use electronics** that were in the smoke until they have been cleaned. Soot is conductive and corrosive.
- **Do not wash clothing** with regular detergent. Smoke-damaged textiles need specialized cleaning or they smell forever.
- **Do not throw things away yet.** Your adjuster and restoration company need to document them, and more is salvageable than you think.
- **Do not sign a contract with the first company that shows up.** Fire scenes attract contractors. Take a card. Make the call yourself.

## What happens next: the restoration process

Fire damage restoration has three overlapping parts.

**Mitigation.** Board-up, tarping, and removal of firefighting water. Yes, water. The hoses that put out the fire usually create a water damage problem on top of the fire damage, and it needs to be dried immediately.

**Cleaning and deodorizing.** Soot type depends on what burned. Wet smoke from smoldering plastics, dry smoke from fast-burning wood and paper, protein residue from a kitchen fire. Each needs a different cleaning method, and using the wrong one makes it worse. After cleaning, odor is removed from the structure with thermal fogging, hydroxyl generators or ozone. Painting over smoke smell does not work.

**Contents and reconstruction.** Salvageable belongings are inventoried, packed out, cleaned at our facility and stored. Unsalvageable items are documented for your contents claim. Then the structure is rebuilt.

## On the emotional side

Losing a home, even part of one, is a real loss and people grieve it. Kids especially. It is fine to be angry about the insurance process and sad about the things that did not make it. The house can be rebuilt, and we will rebuild it. Give yourself and your family time for the rest.
