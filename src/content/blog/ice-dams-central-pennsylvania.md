Every January the calls start. A ceiling stain that appeared overnight. Water dripping from a window frame. Paint bubbling on an exterior wall. The homeowner assumes the roof has failed. Nine times out of ten, it is an ice dam.

## What an ice dam actually is

Snow on the roof melts from the bottom up because heat is escaping from the house into the attic. The meltwater runs down the roof under the snow until it reaches the eave, which hangs past the heated part of the house and is cold. There it refreezes. Layer by layer, a ridge of ice builds along the edge of the roof.

Once the ridge is high enough, new meltwater backs up behind it. Shingles are designed to shed water running down, not water sitting in a pool. The water works under the shingles, through the roof deck, and into your attic insulation, ceiling and walls.

Central Pennsylvania is ice dam country: cold enough for sustained freezing, snowy enough to keep a load on the roof, and full of older homes with under-insulated attics. Altoona, Johnstown, Ebensburg, Somerset and State College all see it every year.

## Warning signs

- Thick ice along the eaves, especially with large icicles
- Icicles forming behind the gutter or along the soffit rather than off the gutter lip
- Water stains on ceilings near exterior walls
- Water at the top of window frames or running down interior walls
- Ice on the exterior siding below the roofline
- A bare, snow-free strip of roof above a heated room while the eaves stay snowy

## What to do right now if water is coming in

1. **Move belongings and put down buckets or plastic.**
2. **Photograph the ceiling and the ice from the ground** for your insurance claim.
3. **Do not climb on the roof.** It is iced. Every winter someone in our area is seriously hurt trying.
4. **Call us.** We can steam the dam off safely, tarp if needed, and start drying wet insulation and drywall before mold begins. Interior water damage from ice dams is usually covered by insurance. [Here is how the claim process works.](/insurance-claims/)

If you want to try something yourself while you wait: fill a nylon stocking with calcium chloride ice melt and lay it across the dam perpendicular to the eave. It melts a channel that lets water drain.

## Why wet attic insulation is the real problem

The stain on your ceiling is the visible part. Above it, the blown-in or batt insulation is soaked. Wet insulation loses its R-value, stays wet for weeks in a cold attic, and grows mold against the drywall. Drying an attic properly means pulling back or removing wet insulation, drying the deck and framing, and treating for mold. Painting over the stain in April does not solve anything.

## Preventing ice dams for good

Ice dams are caused by heat loss, so the fix is on the inside of the house, not the outside.

**Air sealing.** Warm air leaks into the attic through recessed lights, bath fan housings, the attic hatch, plumbing and wiring penetrations, and the tops of interior walls. Sealing these with foam and caulk is the most effective single step.

**Insulation.** Current recommendations for our climate zone are R-49 to R-60 in the attic, roughly 15 to 18 inches of blown cellulose or fiberglass. Many older homes in the region have six inches or less.

**Ventilation.** Soffit and ridge vents keep the roof deck close to outside temperature so snow does not melt from below. Make sure insulation is not blocking the soffit vents.

**Bath and kitchen fans.** They must exhaust outside, not into the attic. We find fans dumping warm moist air into the attic constantly.

Heat cables along the eaves are a band-aid. They can help on a problem spot but they cost money to run and they do not fix the underlying heat loss.
