Most people who call us about a hoarding situation are not the person living in it. They are a daughter, a brother, a neighbor, a landlord. They are worried, often embarrassed on their loved one's behalf, and usually have already tried a few things that did not work. This is written for them.

## Hoarding is a disorder, not a decision

Hoarding disorder is recognized as a mental health condition. It involves persistent difficulty discarding possessions regardless of their value, driven by a perceived need to save them and distress at the thought of letting go. It is often accompanied by anxiety, depression, or the aftermath of loss or trauma. It gets worse with age, and it is common among older adults living alone.

Understanding this changes the approach. Nobody has ever been shamed or argued out of a hoarding disorder. But people can be supported into safer living, one step at a time.

## When it becomes a safety issue

Clutter becomes a hoarding situation when it interferes with living. Warning signs that it has crossed into a health and safety issue:

- Pathways through rooms are narrow or blocked
- Exits, windows or the stove cannot be reached
- Stacks are unstable and could fall
- The bathroom or kitchen is unusable
- There is evidence of pests, animal waste, or mold
- The home smells of decay, urine or ammonia
- Structural concerns: sagging floors from weight, blocked HVAC
- The resident has fallen, or is at risk of falling

Fire is the most serious danger. Hoarded homes burn fast, and firefighters may not be able to enter. If the resident is elderly or has limited mobility, this is urgent.

## How to start the conversation

- **Lead with concern for them, not the stuff.** "I worry about you falling" lands better than "this place is a disaster."
- **Do not touch anything without permission** during early conversations. Trust is everything.
- **Avoid the word hoarding** at first. Many people find it deeply shaming.
- **Focus on one goal:** a clear path to the bathroom, a usable bed, a working stove.
- **Expect resistance.** It is part of the condition, not stubbornness aimed at you.
- **Involve a professional** if possible. Therapists who specialize in hoarding disorder use cognitive behavioral approaches that genuinely help. Area Agencies on Aging in Cambria, Blair and Somerset counties can point you to resources.

## What a professional cleanout looks like

A good hoarding cleanout company does not show up with a dumpster and a crew of strangers who throw everything out. That approach is traumatic and the situation usually recurs within a year.

Here is how we do it at Havok:

1. **A private walk-through** with the family and, whenever possible, the resident. No judgment. We look at safety hazards, biohazard conditions, and structural issues.
2. **A plan with the resident's input.** What must go for safety, what they want to keep, what can be donated. We work at a pace they can handle.
3. **Sorting with respect.** Items are sorted, not shoveled. Important documents, photographs, cash and valuables turn up in hoarded homes constantly and we set them aside.
4. **Biohazard handling** where needed. Animal waste, rodent infestation, mold and human waste are handled under proper protocols by technicians in PPE.
5. **Deep cleaning and deodorizing.**
6. **Repairs.** Years of accumulation often hide leaks, damaged floors, broken plumbing and pest damage. Our reconstruction team fixes what we find.
7. **Discretion.** Unmarked vehicles on request, and we do not discuss the job with neighbors.

## Preventing recurrence

The cleanout is the beginning, not the end. Ongoing support, whether from a therapist, a support group, a professional organizer, or regular check-ins from family, is what keeps a home safe long-term. Many families set up a monthly visit and a small ongoing decluttering routine with the resident.
